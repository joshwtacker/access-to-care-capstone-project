# Access to Care: Opioid Overdose Risk & Treatment Availability

A county-level analysis of opioid overdose mortality, treatment facility access, and socioeconomic risk across the United States from 2018–2024. Built as a data science capstone project using CDC WONDER, SAMHSA, U.S. Census ACS, and USDA data.

**[View the live dashboard](https://joshwtacker.shinyapps.io/access_to_care_us_counties/)**

---

## What this project examines

Opioid overdose deaths have devastated communities across the U.S. for decades, but the burden is not distributed evenly — and neither are the resources meant to address it. This project asks four questions:

1. How do socioeconomic factors like income, poverty, and unemployment relate to overdose mortality rates at the county level?
2. Are treatment facilities located where overdose deaths are highest, or does placement follow a different logic?
3. Does having more treatment facilities in a county correlate with lower overdose mortality?
4. Which counties face the largest gap between how severe their overdose crisis is and how much treatment is available?

---

## Key findings

**Poverty and unemployment predict overdose risk.** Across 1,887 counties, poverty rate (r = +0.29), unemployment rate (r = +0.23), and median income (r = −0.24) all show statistically significant correlations with overdose mortality (all p < 0.001).

**Facilities are not where the need is highest.** 462 counties fall into a "High Risk, Low Access" quadrant — above-median overdose rates with below-median treatment facility rates. Facility placement appears to follow population density rather than overdose burden.

**Rural counties face compounded disadvantage.** After controlling for income, poverty, and unemployment using USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Codes, rurality independently predicts higher overdose mortality (coefficient = −33.4, p < 0.001). Adding this variable improved model R² from 0.118 to 0.170.

**The access gap is nationwide.** Every U.S. state has a positive gap score — meaning overdose burden exceeds treatment supply everywhere. West Virginia and Kentucky account for 14 of the 25 most at-risk counties. McDowell County, WV scores 91.9 out of 100 on the composite risk index.

## The dashboard

The Shiny app lets you explore these findings interactively without running any code. You can:

- View any metric on a county-level choropleth map (overdose rate, facility rate, gap score, RUCC classification, and more)
- Filter by state and by urban-rural type (metro, non-metro, or rural)
- Browse the top at-risk counties ranked by composite risk score, with a slider to control how many to show
- Search and sort the full county-level dataset

## Running the project

### Python notebooks

Requires Python 3.10+.

```bash
pip install pandas numpy matplotlib seaborn scipy statsmodels scikit-learn requests openpyxl
```

Run the notebooks in this order:

1. `API_Socioeconomic_Data_Pull.ipynb` — pulls Census ACS estimates via API
2. `cleaning_data_2024.ipynb` — cleans CDC WONDER mortality data
3. `Merging_Cleaning_Data.ipynb` — builds the master dataset and merges USDA RUCC codes
4. `analysis.ipynb` — runs all four research questions and generates the output charts

The RUCC merge in step 3 downloads data directly from the USDA ERS website, so internet access is required.

### R Shiny app

Requires R 4.2+.

```r
install.packages(c(
  "shiny", "shinydashboard", "shinyjs",
  "leaflet", "sf", "dplyr", "readr",
  "stringr", "plotly", "DT", "viridis"
))
```

Before running the app for the first time, generate the pre-built map file:

```r
source("build_map_data.R")
```

This simplifies the county shapefile and merges it with the dataset, producing `map_data.rds`. The app loads this file at startup instead of downloading shapefiles at runtime, which is what makes deployment to shinyapps.io feasible.

Then launch with:

```r
shiny::runApp("shiny/")
```

To deploy, upload `app.R`, `server.R`, `ui.R`, and `map_data.rds` together.

## Data sources

**CDC WONDER** — Age-adjusted opioid overdose mortality rates per 100,000 population by county, 2018–2024. Counties with fewer than 10 deaths are suppressed per CDC policy and appear as missing in this dataset.

**U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey** — 5-year estimates for median household income, poverty rate, unemployment rate, and county population.

**SAMHSA Treatment Facility Locator** — Substance abuse treatment facility locations, used to calculate facility counts and rates per 100,000 residents by county.

**USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (2023)** — A 9-category classification of counties from most urban (1 = metro area with 1M+ population) to most rural (9 = completely rural, not adjacent to any metro area).

---

## Metric definitions

**Overdose Rate** — Opioid overdose deaths per 100,000 person-years, age-adjusted, aggregated 2018–2024.

**Facility Rate** — SAMHSA-listed treatment facilities per 100,000 residents.

**Gap Score** — Overdose Rate minus Facility Rate. Since both are per-100,000 figures, they are directly comparable. A county with an overdose rate of 250 and a facility rate of 5 has a gap score of 245 — meaning it has 245 more overdose deaths per 100,000 than it has facilities to address them. Every county in the dataset has a positive gap score.

**Composite Risk Score** — An equal-weighted average of three normalized components: overdose rate, poverty rate, and inverse facility rate (so that fewer facilities = higher score). Each component is scaled 0–1 across all counties before averaging, and the final score is expressed on a 0–100 scale. Counties missing any of the three inputs are excluded.

**RUCC** — USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Code. Used in this project as a covariate in regression models to control for urban-rural differences in both overdose patterns and facility availability.

---

## Limitations

CDC WONDER suppresses death counts for counties with fewer than 10 opioid deaths, which means rural and low-population counties are underrepresented in the mortality data. This likely understates overdose risk in some of the most remote areas.

All findings are correlational. The regression models explain a relatively small share of county-level variance in overdose mortality (R² = 0.170 in the best model), which reflects how multifactorial this problem is. Unmeasured factors — drug supply chains, state-level prescribing policy, cultural and historical context — almost certainly explain a large portion of what remains.

The SAMHSA facility data captures whether a treatment facility exists, not whether it has available capacity, accepts a patient's insurance, has manageable wait times, or is geographically reachable for someone without a car in a rural county.
